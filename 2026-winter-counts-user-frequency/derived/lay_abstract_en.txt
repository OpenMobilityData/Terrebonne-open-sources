Lay summary

The borough's winter figures for rue de Terrebonne come from a counter at one point, Kensington, and they count passages: one bicycle crossing that point in one direction. They do not count people. This note looks at what the counts on four sensors along the street (the borough's counter at Kensington, two citizen sensors at King Edward and Draper, and the City's detector at Girouard) can say about how many people cycle the street in winter and how often, and how the survey run by the Association des piétons et cyclistes de NDG in October and November 2026 compares.

From December 1, 2025 to February 28, 2026 the Kensington counter recorded 3,232 passages: about 42 on a weekday and 20 on a weekend day, with more on mild days and fewer on cold or snowy ones and during the holidays. About 11 of the weekday passages come in matched pairs, a morning trip out and an evening trip back, which is the signature of people commuting. Four in ten passages happen in the dark.

The street is busier at its east end in winter. On days without snow, the sensor at Draper counts 1.3 times what Kensington counts, and the City's detector at Girouard about 1.6 times the Kensington figure for the same direction. Kensington is therefore not the busiest point of the lane in winter, and its count understates the use of the street as a whole. On snow days the citizen sensors and the borough's counter disagree by half or more; either the camera sensors over-count on those days, or cyclists leave the snowed-in lane for the roadway where the borough's counter cannot detect them. The data cannot tell which.

How many people are behind the passages? Without knowing how often each person rides, no counter can say. Two things can be said without any assumption: on March 9, 2026 at least 154 different people cycled past King Edward, and on December 3, 2025 at least 42 cycled past Kensington, if nobody passed more than twice that day. A statistical model that sorts riders into frequent and occasional groups shows that a few dozen frequent riders produce most of the passages, and that the occasional riders are many but poorly counted: the same passages could come from 25 people riding often or 292 riding rarely. Dividing passages by two to get people, as was done with the figure of 8 passages presented as 4 cyclists, is one such assumption, and on the day in question the count contained no return trips to divide.

The survey found 87 people who cycled on Terrebonne last winter, 36 of them at least weekly. The trips they describe amount to roughly 60 per cent of the passages counted at Kensington, so the count and the survey are consistent. The survey reached a large share of the frequent riders and a small share of the occasional ones, which is what a voluntary survey of users does. The 87 are a floor under the number of people who cycle the street in winter, not a measure of it.
